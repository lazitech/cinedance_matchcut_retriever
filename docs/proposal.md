Berkeley and World Cinema Through Match Cuts
Our project is an artistic short film connecting everyday life at Berkeley with cinema from different places and times. Through match cuts based on similar expressions, movements, shapes, and compositions, campus scenes lead into movie scenes that suggest shared experiences across cultures and eras. The specific theme is still open and will develop through scripting and footage selection. An assisted editing pipeline will support the film by helping us find matching shots and turn them into transitions.
Creating a match cut usually involves searching for suitable footage and manually adjusting the timing and framing. Chen et al.’s Match Cutting: Finding Cuts with Smooth Visual Transitions (WACV 2023) provides a framework for retrieving shot pairs based on character framing and motion. Our project builds on this work to explore matches involving buildings and everyday objects, organize several candidates around a common visual feature, and turn selected matches into finished sequences.
The main creative contribution is a film that uses visual similarities to connect Berkeley with a wider world. Technically, our goal is to find groups of related shots that offer several editing choices. Retrieval will combine pretrained visual features with shape comparisons and, where useful, motion cues. Manual region selection and candidate review will guide the process. After choosing cut points, 2D transformations can align the matching elements. Our “Anchor-Lock and Release” effect keeps an element near the same position and size at the cut, then gradually restores the incoming shot’s original framing. Starting with clear subjects and modest framing adjustments gives us a practical way to test the idea.
The schedule runs from the November proposal to the December 15 final submission. Our target for the first check-in is one complete transition, followed by a working retrieval-and-alignment prototype for the second. The short film should be ready for the December 8 and 10 presentations. Required resources include campus recording equipment, movie footage, compute for pretrained models, OpenCV, and video editing tools. A small initial library and manual curation should keep the workload manageable.
Evaluation will cover retrieval relevance, alignment, and the viewing experience. For retrieval, we will check whether top-ranked candidates share the query’s shape, composition, or motion. Object position and size before and after alignment will provide a simple geometric comparison. Classmates and friends will also rate direct cuts, retrieval-only cuts, and full-pipeline results for continuity, surprise, and visual appeal in a randomized, unlabeled comparison, and report whether they noticed the cut. Our goal is a preference for the full results over direct cuts, with continuity at least comparable to retrieval-only cuts and greater surprise. A few convincing transitions requiring little manual correction would provide early evidence of feasibility.
So far, our exploration has involved two or three short clips, with manual shot selection and tests of cropping, 2D transformations, and cut points. Initial experiments also include Anchor-Lock and Release. There is no complete end-to-end result yet; these trials are helping us identify practical matches and potential framing problems.
The next step is to test basic retrieval on a small candidate library and make one or two complete transitions. Comparing these with direct cuts should help us decide which components to improve before expanding to more scenes. Object-shaped reveals and beat-synchronized cuts are possible extensions if time permits.
Our data sources are self-recorded campus videos and a small subset of CineDance-1M, subject to checking access and suitability. Campus recordings will include people, buildings, and everyday objects, while the candidate library provides different settings to match against. Shot segmentation and frame sampling will prepare the footage for retrieval, with object masks and motion information extracted as needed. Because resolution, lighting, occlusion, and camera movement vary, the initial selection will favor clear subjects and stable visual anchors. A manually reviewed evaluation set will support comparisons.
