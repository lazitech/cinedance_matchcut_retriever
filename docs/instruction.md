CS180/280A: Intro to Computer Vision and Comp. Photography

Fall 2026 - Final Project Instructions

Everyone is required to do a final project in groups of 4 which includes a project proposal, two midpoint check-ins, a project presentation, and a final artifact (e.g., report, app, webpage, video, or interactive installation). Take the proposal seriously! Your proposal will evolve over the course of check-ins, using the knowledge and results of each previous version to iterate. A strong project requires going through multiple iterations, and with AI, the iteration cycles are getting shorter. These projects go one of only two ways:
You propose something very complex, with many intermediate steps, and it almost always fails.  And even if it doesn’t fail, the results aren’t that exciting. 
You propose something utterly simple, but focus on a cool problem, cool idea, or cool results. These projects are likely to succeed.   
So, remember, think technically simple, but cool or aesthetically pleasing. 

AI Usage: In the first half of this course, you manually coded each of the projects. For the final project, we will be lifting this restriction and encourage AI usage for coding tasks. You will still be responsible for the technical details, and should be able to explain the core technical aspects of your project. 

Iterations and Check-ins: In the past, implementation and testing an idea was often the most expensive and time consuming portion of a project. Because AI has reduced iteration time significantly, you will be required to do a 5-10 minute check-in with the GSIs at least 2 times. Your group will be expected to bring an updated proposal with substantive results to discuss at the check-in (see “Proposal Format”).

Data Selection: Think very carefully about the available data for your problem. Beyond technical feasibility, do you have the amount of quality data required to have a successful project? Most projects succeed or fail based on the quality of data available!

Grading: This project is intended to build a portfolio of work that will help you in the real world, e.g. in getting a job. To this end, we will be gauging your projects on how "cool" / compelling they are. The check-ins are designed to help your project meet this bar. Here are some characteristics common in cool projects:

Cool projects tend to have a very simple idea at the core, executed well. 
You can show the results and explain it to your nontechnical grandparent in a few minutes, and they will be truly compelled. You've made it so that it can be appreciated by a broad audience.
You can show the results and explain it to an engineering hiring manager at a company, and they will understand it enough to be truly impressed with your contribution. This goes to your technical and/or creative prowess, and excellence in communicating what you did. The reaction to aim for is: “Wow, I want to learn more about this!”.
In explaining it, you can speak authoritatively and fluently about the relevant prior work. You actively teach how to understand what you started from and what your human contribution was. This goes to scholarship that has made you knowledgeable, and commitment to making others understand what you did and didn't do.
In following up on your project, as staff will in grading your report and a hiring manager would before offering you a job, the details of your project are consistent with the story you've presented. That is, you championed what is excellent about your project, but you didn't oversell it. This goes to your credibility and reputation.

Staff grading of "coolness" will be an inherently subjective judgment, as it is in the real world. We will make a collective, subjective ranking of projects into buckets. There is no inherent reward for a time-consuming or complicated project if the final results are not compelling; on the other hand, as the old adage goes, creativity is 1% inspiration and 99% perspiration, so a highly successful project will surely involve a lot of human work -- iterative application of your creativity, experimentation, active learning, judgment, critical thinking, taste and resourcefulness.

Proposal Format: For each check-in, you will be required to update your existing proposal based on the results of initial experiments demonstrating the feasibility of your idea. Projects often go through multiple iterations, and it is perfectly acceptable and expected to have negative results especially during the early iterations. What’s more important is using your findings to design the next iteration.

The proposal should be a short document pitching the idea. You should have about 1 page of text, and two pages of images or figures that are necessary to communicate the idea and data. The document should address the following questions (an augmented subset of DARPA’s Heilmeier Catechism):
What are you trying to do? Articulate your objectives using absolutely no jargon. 
How is it done today, and what are the limits of current practice? (If it’s total new, motivate why it will be cool to do) 
What is new in your approach and why do you think it will be successful? (What is the specific novelty that you think you’ll address and what’s the insight you want to explore. Why do you have a hope of succeeding?) 
How long will it take? (Is this feasible? Are there resources you need?)
How do you measure success? (How will you know if you have succeeded? Will you get advanced warning?) 
What have you tried so far (covering lessons from previous proposals), and what do the results say about your idea? 
What are your next steps? (Think of the minimal next experiment to run to give direction)
What data will you work with, and where are you getting it from? Be sure to discuss the quality of data and what information is contained within it.
Your proposal should not address these questions one by one but instead tell a narrative.

You should meet with a GSI during office hours to get checked off for credit.

Final Report: The deliverables are different between 180 and 280A. For 180 projects, you will be expected to produce a high quality blog post, app, report, or video, explaining the problem, your methodology/data, and any unique insights you derived through the project.

280A projects will be required to write a 4 page, high quality workshop-style paper, and are highly encouraged to produce a corresponding project website with visualizations.

Final Presentation: Every team will do a two minute final presentation. These will be held at the usual lecture time and location during RRR week. Attendance is expected, and is a good chance to support and learn more about everyone’s projects!

Mixed projects will be held to the 280A standard.

Can we do more than 2 check-ins with the GSIs?
	Yes! This is encouraged, but we only require 2 for credit. 

Do all group members need to go to the check-in?
	At least 2 group members need to be at the check-in.

Do we need to do something technically novel?
	A cool project doesn’t have to only be technically novel. A project that is technically simple, but incredibly creative or artistic also works.

Can we have a group of more or less than 4?
	No, unless you really can’t find another team member / another way and you make a private post on Ed and receive permission.
